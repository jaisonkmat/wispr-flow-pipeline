Stage 0: the decision to invoke

Never measured

Happens in your head, before any code runs

Nothing starts until the user decides to invoke Flow and reaches for the hotkey. If past experience taught them the tool is slow or clips their first words, they hesitate and type instead.

Wispr names this problem themselves: habit formation is one of the eleven open problems on their engineering blog.

The vocabulary

TermWhat it means here
InvokeThe act of activating Flow: deciding to speak and pressing the hotkey. Time-to-invoke is the gap between wanting to say something and the system actually listening.
ASR / STTAutomatic speech recognition, also called speech-to-text. Audio in, raw words out. Stage 2, and Wispr trains its own.

The 700ms budget

Measured from the moment you stop speaking. The figures are Sahaj Garg's, from Wispr's engineering blog. Wispr engineering, Sep 2025

The LLM figure varies by source: under 200ms on Wispr's blog, 250ms for 100 tokens in the Baseten case study and on SE Radio. Read it as roughly 200 to 250ms.

The parts do not fill the whole. The three ceilings sum to 600ms against the 700ms target, and no published source itemizes the remainder. These are separate engineering requirements rather than an allocation: "a maximum networking budget of 200ms" most naturally covers the full network round trip. The unclaimed margin is headroom in a system whose workloads are inherently variable.
Why the worst case and not the average. Users judge a tool by its worst moment, not its typical one. When people report Flow "feels slow," the team looks at the distribution and almost always finds one or two outliers driving the whole impression. A product that is fast 98 percent of the time still feels unreliable, and unreliable kills the habit. The budget is a soft target on the P50 and P99 distributions rather than a per-request guarantee, because model workloads are inherently variable. SE Radio 703

Stage by stage

Each stage carries what happens, the engineering underneath it, and how it fails. Expand what you need.

Stage 0: the decision to invoke

Cognitive, not technical. Invisible to telemetry.

What actually happens

Before a single line of code executes, the user decides whether this thought is worth invoking Flow for. That decision is made from memory: how well did it go last time, did it clip the first words, did the output need fixing.

Sahaj defines latency as the elapsed time from when a user tries to do something to when they experience the response. SE Radio 703 Under that definition, hesitation before the keypress is latency. It just does not appear on any dashboard, because measurement starts at the keypress.

Time-to-invoke is the name for this gap: the distance between wanting to say something and the system actually listening. It sits upstream of time-to-send, the metric the pipeline is engineered against, and it is the gap that decides whether the pipeline gets used at all.

Why it matters to the product

Habit formation is one of the eleven open problems on Wispr's engineering blog: teaching a broader audience to speak rather than type requires UX and design innovation to build a sustained voice habit. Wispr engineering

The connection to this stage: every bad experience downstream, a clipped opening, a slow outlier, a wrong transcription, gets paid for here, as hesitation on the next invoke. A user who has been burned develops a reflex to type instead, precisely in the high-urgency moments where voice input is most valuable. The habit that makes Flow sticky, users dictating 72 percent of their characters after six months, is built or broken at this stage.

Stage 1: the microphone wakes up

Local. macOS Core Audio. Variable by hardware setup.

What actually happens

On the keypress, the client has to resolve the audio environment before it can capture anything: identify the active input device, negotiate with Core Audio, confirm sample rate and routing, and establish a clean stream. Missing opening words is a common enough outcome that the help center has a dedicated article on it. Wispr help center

On a built-in laptop microphone this is close to instant. The more hardware sits between the speaker and the system, the longer and less consistent the negotiation: Bluetooth headsets renegotiating their audio profile, external USB or XLR interfaces, virtual audio devices, or multi-input setups where the wrong device can win the selection. If the stream is not fully ready when speech starts, the opening syllables are simply not captured.

Why quality fluctuates: the audio, not the model

Sahaj wrote an entire post on this, and its thesis is the single most useful sentence in Flow debugging: "It's rarely the model itself. Much more often, it's a change in the audio." Wispr engineering, Jan 2026

His named causes, all of which live in this stage:

  • Microphone changes. "Bluetooth mics like AirPods often have worse audio quality and can clip the beginning or end of speech."
  • Phantom pairing. "Sometimes, bluetooth headphones can pair even when they're in your pocket. There's basically no audio."
  • System settings. One of Wispr's longest internal "the model is broken" scares turned out to be a macOS microphone input volume set too low.
  • Environment. Background noise or nearby conversations can be picked up as unintended speech.
  • Comfort drift. As people get used to Flow, they mumble more, speak more quietly, and dictate half-awake or lying down. The audio degrades because trust went up.

The last one is worth sitting with: it means transcription quality can decline because the product is working. A complaint of "it got worse over time" can be the user's own speech relaxing, not a regression.

The built-in isolator: listen to the audio

Flow's history keeps the recorded audio of each dictation, and Wispr's own guidance is to download and listen to it when quality drops. Wispr engineering

This is the cleanest diagnostic in the whole pipeline, because it splits stage 1 from stage 2 with one action. If the recording sounds clipped, quiet, or empty, the problem is capture: this stage. If the recording sounds fine and the text is still wrong, the problem is downstream in the models. One listen eliminates half the system.

Stage 1.5: the trip to the server

Network and infrastructure. Runs while you are still talking.

Correct the mental model first. Your audio does not sit on your laptop waiting for you to finish. The connection opens and audio flows up in small chunks while you are mid-sentence, so by the time you stop speaking, the server already holds nearly all of it. That is why the 700ms budget starts when you stop: the upload was paid underneath your sentence, and what remains is the final chunk, the model work, and the return trip. Whether the ASR transcribes incrementally during speech or in one fast pass at commit is not publicly documented; either way, the under-200ms ceiling covers whatever transcription work remains once you stop.

The 700ms budget
The six things that happen before a model hears you
  1. Address lookup. Hostname to server address. Normally cached and near-instant, but a cold lookup on a bad network is real time.
  2. Secure connection. A TLS handshake plus a WebSocket upgrade. A WebSocket is a pipe that stays open in both directions: pay the setup cost once, then push audio through continuously instead of making a fresh request per chunk.
  3. The auth message. Sent first. Carries the access token, plus language, app metadata, and conversation context. Wispr API docs That context is not decoration: it is what lets the cleanup model know you are in Slack and not in an email.
  4. Region routing. Customer data stays inside US infrastructure, with model inference running on AWS through Baseten. Wispr security FAQ Baseten
  5. The append stream. Audio chunks flow up as you talk. Each carries a position number so the server can detect a dropped or out-of-order packet, plus a volume reading.
  6. The commit message. You stopped talking. It reports the total packet count so the server can confirm it received everything. This is the starting gun for the 700ms.

One caveat: this protocol is documented for Wispr's developer platform, the API third parties build on. The desktop app very likely speaks the same or a closely related protocol, but that is not publicly confirmed.

What is actually in the pipe

Audio is sent as single-channel 16-bit PCM at 16kHz, base64 encoded. Wispr API docs

Unpacked: one microphone channel, not stereo. 16,000 samples per second, which is plenty for speech since the human voice lives well below the 8kHz ceiling that rate gives you. Each sample is 2 bytes, so roughly 32KB of raw audio per second, about a quarter of a megabit. Trivially small. Bandwidth is not the constraint here, round trips are.

There is an option to switch the encoding to MessagePack, a compact binary format, to cut bandwidth further. Classic latency engineering: shrink the payload so the pipe drains faster on a weak connection.

Chunks must be a consistent duration and positions must increment correctly. Break either rule and the server cannot reassemble the audio in order, which surfaces as dropped or garbled words that have nothing to do with the models.

What the server decides once your audio lands

Batch or run alone. A GPU is far more efficient processing several requests together. Waiting a beat for a second request raises throughput and lowers cost, but adds queue time to yours. This is the permanent tension in inference serving: one user's latency against everyone's efficiency.

Request shape makes batching harder at scale. Sahaj's example: one request is a long input with a short output, another is the reverse. The GPU now has to do two different things at once, and the way it parallelizes changes. SE Radio 703

Is a GPU even warm. Inference autoscales and scales to zero when idle. Baseten Good for cost, but a request arriving at a cold moment pays a startup cost that a mid-traffic request does not. Exactly the kind of thing that lives in the P99 and never in the average.

Orchestration. The speech model and the Llama cleanup model run as a multi-step chain using Baseten's Chains framework, with the Llama step compiled via TensorRT-LLM. Baseten

The pattern worth carrying: this stage produces failures that look like model failures. A dropped chunk looks like the speech model mishearing. Isolating which stage broke matters more than guessing at causes.

Stage 2: the ASR model transcribes

First of two models. Budget: under 200ms. Wispr trains its own.

What actually happens

The speech recognition model converts audio into raw text, handling accent, pace, and ambient noise. What comes out is faithful but messy: the ums, the false starts, the abandoned sentence are all still there.

This is a dedicated speech model, separate from the LLM that follows. The two run in sequence, not as a single pass, so their delays stack rather than overlap.

Why transcription is inherently ambiguous

Wispr's research post makes an argument most people miss: industry word error rates improving from 3 percent to 2 percent have "hardly made a dent" on the hard cases, because the audio itself is inherently ambiguous. No amount of listening harder resolves it. Wispr engineering, Sep 2025

The named categories of ambiguity:

  • Phonetic ambiguity. In whispered speech, voiced and unvoiced sounds are acoustically identical.
  • Homophones and short utterances. "Lauren" and "Laurent" are virtually indistinguishable by sound alone.
  • Proper nouns. Getting a name or a podcast title right requires world knowledge, not better hearing.
  • Domain vocabulary. Medical and legal terminology is underrepresented in training data.
  • Accents. A strong accent can trick a model into thinking you switched languages entirely.
  • Logical coherence. A pure acoustic model will happily output "gabapentin 903 times a day," which no human transcriber would ever write.

The conclusion Wispr draws: transcription is not an audio problem, it is a context problem. Their ASR is conditioned on speaker qualities, surrounding context, and individual history, so the model resolves ambiguity the way a human assistant would, by knowing you. Wispr engineering

This is also why very large custom dictionaries can hurt accuracy: every added term is another candidate the model must weigh on every utterance. Wispr engineering

Why the cloud and not your laptop

The industry trend is shrinking models onto the device. Sahaj describes that option and rejects it for this use case: you can quantize a small model onto Apple Silicon and it "will produce super fast responses but they won't be very good." SE Radio 703

The reasoning is a time calculation, in his words: "if the user has to go back and fix up their transcript or edit a mistake in the speech recognition, that experience is no longer better than typing."

A network trip costs milliseconds. A correction costs seconds of stopping, clicking, fixing, and finding your place. So Wispr spends the milliseconds and buys the accuracy, running large models in the cloud and training their own speech models.

100+ languages, and code-switching

Flow supports over 100 languages, with seven at what Wispr calls English-level performance: French, German, Hindi, Italian, Portuguese, Spanish, and Thai. Wispr engineering, Jan 2026

The engineering behind that number:

  • Dynamic engine selection. Flow selects the most accurate ASR engine per language, which cut transcription error rates by more than half.
  • Accent confidence scoring. Compares candidate transcriptions to prevent a strong accent from being misread as a different language.
  • Code-switching support. Roughly half the world speaks more than one language day to day. Hinglish speakers blend Hindi and English in one sentence and prefer romanized script; Flow outputs romanized Hindi without switching scripts mid-sentence.

Code-switching within a single utterance remains one of the eleven named open challenges, which is a useful calibration: shipped does not mean solved.

Stage 3: the LLM cleans it up

Second model. A fine-tuned Llama, compiled with TensorRT-LLM. Budget: roughly 200 to 250ms.

What actually happens

A fine-tuned Llama takes the messy transcript and rewrites it into what you meant: punctuation, paragraph breaks, filler removed, restarts repaired. It also shapes output to the app you are in, using the context sent up in the auth message. Baseten

The published performance target is 100 or more tokens generated in under 250ms, measured at the tail.

Everything before this point is transcription. This step is interpretation, and it is what separates Flow from plain dictation.

The strict rule: output stays the size of input

Sahaj: "we're really careful to make sure that the responses that we produce are very similar size to the input. It's actually a very strict requirement that we have." SE Radio 703

The reason is not performance, it is authorship. People do not want the model "expanding on your behalf and deciding what you want to say on your behalf." The user must experience that they chose the words.

This is a product philosophy expressed as an engineering constraint, and it is the cleanest example in the system of the two being the same decision.

Short form versus long form

Short messages like Slack and texts: filler words such as "like" and "though" are load-bearing. They carry tone. Stripping them makes the writer sound colder than they are, so they are preserved. The documented version of this: in supported messaging apps, Flow drops trailing periods for a more casual feel, tuned by the Writing Style setting. Wispr help center

Long form like email: nobody speaks a precisely structured email for a minute straight. Here the model breaks text into paragraphs, structures it, and lightly edits, with the constraint that the user can still scan the output and confirm no meaning was lost. SE Radio 703

Getting this wrong in either direction, over-editing a casual text or under-editing a rambling email, breaks the feeling that Flow understands you.

Speculative decoding, and what it bought

Normally a language model generates one token at a time, each pass feeding the next. For a cleanup task that is wasteful, because most of the output is identical to the input.

Speculative decoding guesses that the next stretch of output matches the input and verifies the guess in a single pass, dropping into normal generation only where an edit is actually needed. "We should, um, launch on Tuesday, wait no, Wednesday morning" keeps "We should launch on" verbatim and only generates the corrected ending.

This cut LLM latency by over half. The savings were not banked as speed: they "allowed us actually budget for running even more models as part of our workflow." SE Radio 703

The principle: recovered milliseconds get reinvested in capability. The budget is a spending plan, not a description.

Personalization: learned on your machine

The models run in the cloud, but most personalization data must live on the user's device, for both privacy and latency. Wispr describes capturing edits locally, deciding whether an edit should generalize and in which contexts, and learning a local policy aligned to the user's style. Wispr engineering

Why it is hard: writing style differs at the token level, in punctuation and capitalization habits, and language models have strong recall but poor precision for exactly that kind of nuance. The formatter also learns regional conventions from user edits, like French spacing before punctuation and German quotation marks. Wispr engineering

So the architecture is not cloud versus device. It is big models in the cloud, your profile on your machine.

How this stage fails

Sahaj describes the pathologies directly: a model can go off the rails, start answering a different question, or repeat the same sentence indefinitely so the request never terminates. SE Radio 703

Three defenses he names: length safeguards, since knowing roughly how long the output should be caps how bad it can get, and dictation has a natural cap because output must match input size; mid-generation detection, catching a runaway while it is still being produced; and a checker, heuristics or a second model watching the output.

Worth noting for debugging: a runaway generation here and a dropped packet in stage 1.5 can both surface to the user as "garbled output." Entirely different bugs, at entirely different stages.

Stage 4: the text lands in your app

Back across the network, into the active text field. Desktop: clipboard paste, then restore. iOS: keyboard. Android: Flow Bubble.

What actually happens: paste, then restore

On Mac and Windows, Flow inserts text through the clipboard: "Flow temporarily uses your clipboard to paste text and restores your previous contents after a successful paste." Wispr help center The transcript goes onto the clipboard, a paste lands it in the active field, and the previous clipboard contents come back. Insertion requires an active cursor in a text field, and on Mac the Accessibility permission.

The mechanism differs by platform. On iOS, Flow operates as a keyboard, so text appears directly in the field without touching the clipboard. On Android, the Flow Bubble inserts directly into most apps, with a copy button as the fallback when direct insertion fails.

What gets inserted is plain text: numbered lists, punctuation, line breaks. Smart Formatting structures speech into text like "1. Finish the report," not into markup symbols. Wispr help center

How this stage fails, per the documentation

Every documented failure at this stage is about delivery: the text is finished and correct, and the handoff into the host application breaks.

The built-in safety net: the transcript is always on the clipboard after a dictation, so a manual paste recovers it, and history holds the text regardless.

The debugging value of this stage is isolation. Same audio, same models, different destination: if dictation lands clean in one application and fails in another, the failure is in delivery, not in capture or transcription. Flow's own local logs mirror this, treating insertion as a first-class error category with classes like INSERTION / PASTE, which matches the documented paste mechanism.

The gap after the pipeline

The pipeline ends at insertion, but the user's task does not. Tanay Kothari notes there is still roughly half a second between the text appearing and the user pressing enter.

The "press enter" voice command closes that gap by keeping the user inside the voice modality instead of returning them to the keyboard. Small, but it compounds: it removes the last modality switch in the loop.

Notice the symmetry across the whole document: an unmeasured gap before the pipeline, the time-to-invoke, and an unmeasured gap after it, the time-to-confirm. The instrumented 700ms sits between two moments that only exist in user behavior.

Scale, and why uptime sits on that list

As of September 2025, users dictated 1 billion words a month, with 10x growth expected within six months, at a 99.99 percent uptime requirement. Wispr engineering Four nines is roughly four minutes of allowed downtime a month.

The difficulty is holding availability and speed at once. A service can always buy uptime with slowness by queueing everyone, and can always buy speed by dropping requests it cannot serve fast. Neither escape is available here. And scale does not merely enlarge the problem: systems look fine for a long time, then cross a threshold that exposes an entirely new source of latency. One CUDA kernel flag once changed performance by 25 percent under one specific load pattern, found only through observation. SE Radio 703

Related and easy to miss: Wispr runs no latency regression testing. They rely on per-event analytics, gradual rollouts, and fast detection, because these workloads are too stochastic to simulate in staging. Production telemetry and user reports are the regression test.

Where the pipeline is headed

Wispr describes the progression as moving from the one habit people perform a hundred times a day, dictation, to the ten things they do ten times a day: messaging without switching context, iteratively editing a draft by voice, asking questions mid-workflow. Eventually an extension store for the long tail, once voice UX conventions are defined. Wispr engineering, Sep 2025

The stated bar for those actions is reliability, not capability: an assistant that messages the wrong contact even 20 percent of the time is one you stop trusting. The same trust logic that governs the decision to invoke governs every future capability.
